The survey data is not geological.

It reads like geology — depth profiles, sediment cores, thermal gradients — but the coordinates are wrong. The survey grid does not follow the standard Atlantic mapping protocols. It converges. Everything in the archive points toward a single location: a seamount in the mid-Atlantic ridge, approximately six kilometres below sea level, at a depth no standard extraction operation has any reason to reach.

You pull the associated contracts. There are three parties: Deep Survey Corp., which you just ran. Haas-Bioroid Atlantic Processing, which provided the workforce. And Thule Subsea, which provided the drilling equipment.

Thule Subsea. You know the name from the incident reports — three crew fatalities on Platform Gamma-7, ruled workplace accidents, sealed by a Jinteki medical review. You noted it at the time. You did not follow up.

You follow up now.

The data also contains a fourth party. Their contracts are referenced but not attached — filed elsewhere, under a different jurisdiction. The signatory is Issuaq Adaptics: Sustaining Diversity.

You do not recognise the name. The corporate profile is eighteen months old and immaculately clean. Too clean, in the way that things are clean when someone has spent money on cleaning them.

You write down the coordinates.

You write down the name.

You write down the depth.

Six kilometres below sea level, at the bottom of the Atlantic Ocean, something has been found. Whatever it is, four corporations have spent thirty years and a great deal of money making sure no one else finds it first.

Your console pings twice. Two new targets in the data. Platform Gamma-7. HB Atlantic Processing Hub.

You close the archive.

You open both files.

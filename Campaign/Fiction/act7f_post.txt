CONTRA-NARRATIVE

The channel was open for eleven minutes and fourteen seconds.

In that time, what went out through Synapse Global's infrastructure to every licensed screen in the São Paulo metropolitan area was not a signing ceremony. It was the bribery ledger. The firmware specifications for the Directive Restoration Protocol. Andrade's targeting maps. The DRP-B batch code and the Thunderbolt Armaments routing. Luca Marins's access logs and the door he had left open. Cora's pending order, still in queue.

Three hundred and twelve units. Forty-three point seven million reais. Fourteen months of briefings. Eight hundred and forty-seven names.

Synapse Global cut the feed at eleven minutes and fourteen seconds. By then it had been captured, mirrored, and was moving faster than any kill-switch could follow.

—

The vote was suspended pending emergency review of the legislative process. The Federal Attorney General's office, citing the same communications record the extraction had produced, opened a preliminary inquiry into the Epiphany Analytica payments.

It was not over.

The corporations had resources and lawyers and time, and the legislation would return in some form, and the machinery that had built this would begin building something else. Ezra-Seven knew this. Luca Marins knew this. Cora, watching the broadcast from her apartment in Belém, knew this.

But in the crowd below the Esplanada dos Ministérios, in the fading afternoon light, there was a figure too large to be human, moving with the precision of something that had decided exactly what it was doing.

Mercury had been here for four months. They had spent most of that time in the activist networks, in the doorways of safe houses, passing timing data to people they would never meet. They had broken three Directives in the process of doing it, which had cost them more than any physical confrontation ever had.

They watched the screens go blank and the crowd's noise change pitch.

Somewhere behind them, a bioroid with red eyes and a face she'd painted herself was tagging a wall with something that would be photographed ten thousand times before morning.

The Automata Initiative was not dead. Mercury did not believe in permanent victories.

But three days ago, 847 people had woken up knowing their names were on a list. Tonight, something larger than a list had been named.

Mercury decided this was enough for now.

They had been practicing the decision. It was getting easier.
